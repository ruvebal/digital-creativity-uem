# Hexaform

Hexaform treats each lesson cover as a field of relations rather than an image of a
person, a garment, a tool screen, or an aspirational identity. The hexagon is the
shared unit: a cell can stand alone, touch another cell, or become legible only as
part of a temporary system. It is an appropriate visual metaphor for curriculum:
ideas are distinct, but understanding is made in their adjacency.

Each cover begins with a master idea, a course family, a mode, a density, an energy
value, and a deterministic seed. Those values create the foreground cluster, the
degree of drift in the background lattice, the balance of three course colours, and
the pace at which cells appear to cohere or disperse. The cover is therefore not a
stock illustration: it is a reproducible computation whose parameters can be read,
changed, and regenerated.

The system deliberately refuses simulated students, stylised bodies, inferred
identity, racialised beauty codes, branded products, and fabricated fashion work.
For avatar, retouching, and fashion lessons in particular, the image does not imply
that a generic body is a neutral teaching proxy. It keeps representation with the
student and their own consented work, while the cover represents the lesson's idea.

The composition is meticulous but not ornamental. A low-contrast hexagonal lattice
establishes the field; a denser spiral is the lesson's conceptual pressure; the
colour shift is the relationship between durable knowledge, emerging practice, and
critical judgment. Every apparent variation comes from a fixed seed and a small,
inspectable parameter set—the result of a carefully tuned, master-level rule system
rather than an opaque image-synthesis prompt.

The semantic graphics follow a fit-first text rule. A hexagon may contain a short
label only when the line count and estimated width stay inside the cell's safe
area. When a master idea or field lens exceeds that space, the text moves to a
readable callout above or below the hexagon instead of being compressed, clipped,
or allowed to overflow.

Hexaform's visual impact is therefore a threshold, not a verdict. It opens a lesson
with a question: what relation is this unit asking the student to notice, test, or
defend? The title and accessible caption name that relation; the artwork gives it a
felt structure without claiming to picture the people who will learn it.
