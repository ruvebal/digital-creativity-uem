# D2 · Transposition

**Digital Creativity I · research and projects · 10% of the course grade**

> **Submit before 23 November 2026 through Campus Virtual.** Campus Virtual is the binding source for the exact submission time and file channel.

## The assignment in one sentence

Make a **cartel / poster image** from a work in another language: a song, film, poem, literary work, theatre piece, or comparable source. An image series is welcome when it works as one coherent visual proposal.

D2 is the same bilingual brief as **ACT3 Transposición**. It is one deliverable, not a second task for another cohort.

## What “transposition” means here

Do not illustrate the source or decorate it with borrowed imagery. Identify what the source does — its tension, rhythm, point of view, atmosphere, conflict, or question — then make visual decisions that give it a new form.

Your image must be able to stand on its own while making the source relationship legible. Composition, colour, hierarchy, typography, material treatment, and scale are part of the argument.

### Two working propositions

> “A transposition occurs when a genre or a particular textual product changes support or language.”
>
> “Each transposition involves a choice about resignifying the work.”

— Óscar Steimberg, course translations reproduced from the Creativity Techniques D2 brief.

For D2, cross at least one **language/code** or **support**: for example, a song’s temporal rhythm into a still image, a poem’s voice into typographic hierarchy, or a film scene into a poster’s visual argument. A filter, a new colour palette, or a copied scene alone is not enough. Ask: **what can the image now make possible, and what does it leave behind?**

## Work through the movement

Treat the brief as a sequence of decisions, not a last-minute export:

1. **Frame the source.** Select one element worth carrying across: a conflict, gesture, fragment, rhythm, image, or question. Record the source and its credit before you make.
2. **Name the crossing.** State what changes: language/code, medium or genre, support/material carrier, audience context — or more than one of these.
3. **Set one formal constraint.** Choose a rule that makes the visual argument sharper: a restricted palette, a single type family, one image treatment, a scale rule, an analogue/digital limitation, or another declared constraint. A constraint should remove easy options and force a decision.
4. **Prototype and test.** Make an early version; show it to a peer or intended viewer; record what they notice first and whether it matches your intended reading.
5. **Revise and account for it.** Keep an alternative, rejection, or change of direction. Explain what the test taught you and what you decided to keep, change, or refuse.

> “Creativity does not mean improvisation without method: that way only produces confusion…”
>
> — Bruno Munari, course translation reproduced from the Creativity Techniques D2 brief.

## The short critical note

Submit a **250–350 word critical note** with the work (or a 2–3 minute captioned audio/video equivalent). Address all four prompts:

1. What did you carry across from the source, what did you omit or alter, and why?
2. Where does the work act as **language/code**, **medium/genre**, and **support/material or file**? Do not treat those as the same thing.
3. What reading did you intend? What did one viewer actually read or notice first? Identify one productive gap and the revision or refusal it produced.
4. Whose work, values, labour, images, data, or tools are visible in the result — and what responsibility follows from that?

## What to submit

Upload the final work and a compact process package through Campus Virtual:

- **Final cartel / poster image**, or a coherent image series, exported clearly enough to inspect.
- **Editable working file or accessible working link**, when the chosen tool permits it.
- **Source declaration:** title, author/maker, date where known, format, and a usable source/credit record.
- **Short critical note:** answer the four prompts above.
- **Visual research and process trail:** references, sketches, declared constraint, early tests, one viewer reading, alternatives, and at least one revision or rejected route.
- **Authorship, material, and AI declaration** for this piece. Follow the [AI usage declaration]({{ '/ai-declaration/' | relative_url }}): record **Tools used**, **Purpose of use**, **Human contribution**, and **Source material**. “No AI tools used” is a valid declaration.

Credit all source material and name collaborators where applicable. A source credit is not permission to copy: transform, test, and make your own visual argument.

| --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Transposition and critical idea** | 30% | A precise, surprising visual argument crosses language/code or support and transforms the source rather than illustrating it. | The crossing and visual idea are clear. | The source is identifiable but the transformation is literal or thin. | No legible crossing, or copied/undeclared material replaces an idea. |
| **Visual communication** | 25% | Composition, hierarchy, colour, typography, and material choices make the intended reading compelling. | Visual choices are coherent and readable. | Core message is readable but choices are generic, uneven, or unresolved. | Communication is confusing, illegible, or unconsidered. |

| **Source, circulation, and credits** | 15% | Source, visual lineage, language/medium/support reading, and credits are exact and critically handled. | Source and key references are credited; relation and intended reading are explained. | Basic source credit is present but relation, circulation, or record is incomplete. | Source is missing, misleading, or uncredited. |
