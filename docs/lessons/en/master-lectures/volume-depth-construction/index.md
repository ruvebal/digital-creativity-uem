> **Perspective is not how eyes see. It is a rule for building pictures — and every rule assumes someone.**

Lecture A showed that the viewer builds volume from cues. This lecture asks **who wrote the rules** that pictures use to organise those cues. ## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture you can:

1. Describe **Alberti's window** and the **visual pyramid** with four terms: station point, rays, picture plane, image.
2. Name the **two assumptions** of central perspective and what they leave out of real vision.
3. Use Panofsky's idea of perspective as a **symbolic form** to compare spatial systems from different cultures.
4. Classify a pictorial invention as **combinational, exploratory or transformational** (Boden).
5. Explain why perfect perspective views **cannot certify** how a garment fits.

## Masterclass · six ideas (40′)

### 1 · The picture as an open window

In Florence in **1435**, Leon Battista Alberti told painters to treat the picture as an open window onto the scene. A decade or so earlier, Filippo Brunelleschi had demonstrated the method with painted panels. Erwin Panofsky, writing in 1927, describes the procedure in the first person:

> I imagine the picture – in accord with the “window” definition – as a planar cross section through the so-called visual pyramid

<p class="quote-ref" markdown="1">— [(Panofsky 1991, 28)](#ref-panofsky-1993)</p>

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-pyramid" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/lesson-figures/visual-forger/vd-b-visual-pyramid-26982e30ab05.svg' | relative_url }}" width="1600" height="900" alt="Course diagram: one still eye on the left; dashed rays run to a panel with a black dress silhouette; a framed picture plane cuts the rays, and a smaller red dress marks the image where the rays cross the window." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption><strong>Alberti's window: a cut through the visual pyramid.</strong> Course diagram · Rubén Vega Balbás · CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

Four terms to keep: the **station point** (the eye), the **visual rays**, the **picture plane** (the window) and the **image** (where the rays cross the window). The plate below shows the measuring tool: a floor of squares converging to one centric point.

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-alberti-grid" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/8b9edff7f4ed9386.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Perspective construction plate after Alberti’s Della pittura: a floor of squares converging to one centric point." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Construction plate after Alberti's <em>Della pittura</em>. Public domain · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Della_Pittura_Alberti_perspective_vanishing_point.jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

### 2 · One still eye, one measurable space

Panofsky points out that this construction makes two “bold abstractions” from real vision: that **we see with a single and immobile eye** [(Panofsky 1991, 29)](#ref-panofsky-1993), and that a flat cut through the visual pyramid can stand for our optical image. Real vision uses two moving eyes and a curved retina. What we gain in exchange is a space we can calculate:

> In a sense, perspective transforms psychophysiological space into mathematical space.

<p class="quote-ref" markdown="1">— [(Panofsky 1991, 31)](#ref-panofsky-1993)</p>

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-piero" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/73bb388bdbc19d65.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Piero della Francesca’s Flagellation: a measured architectural space with a tiled floor, three robed men in the foreground and a scene deep behind." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Piero della Francesca, <em>The Flagellation</em>, about 1459–60. Galleria Nazionale delle Marche, Urbino. Public domain · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Piero_-_The_Flagellation.jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

Piero della Francesca, who wrote a treatise on perspective for painters, measured every tile of this floor. The course perspective kit draws the same contrast:

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-p1" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/lesson-figures/perspective-kit/pv-p1-psycho-vs-math-5e69c5149013.svg' | relative_url }}" width="1600" height="900" alt="Diagram contrasting psychophysiological space with mathematical perspective space." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption><strong>Lived space and mathematical space.</strong> Course perspective kit · Rubén Vega Balbás.</figcaption>
</figure>

Geometry is not the whole story. Around 1472 Leonardo da Vinci painted distant mountains paler and bluer: **aerial perspective** is a perception cue (Lecture A), not a construction rule. Geometry fixes where things sit; atmosphere tells you how far they feel.

### 3 · Not whether perspective, but which

Panofsky's central idea: perspective is a **symbolic form** — a way in which a culture attaches meaning to a concrete, visible sign. So the useful question is never only “is this picture correct?”:

> it is essential to ask of artistic periods and regions not only whether they have perspective, but also which perspective they have

<p class="quote-ref" markdown="1">— [(Panofsky 1991, 40)](#ref-panofsky-1993)</p>

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-uki-e" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/9f3a4cf68ffeb417.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Japanese uki-e woodblock print by Utagawa Toyoharu: an Edo theatre district drawn in imported Western perspective, crowds in kimono receding along the street." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Utagawa Toyoharu, <em>Perspective picture (uki-e): theatre district at dawn on opening day of the kabuki season</em>, late 18th century. The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Public domain · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Perspective_Picture_(Uki-e)-_Theater_District_at_Dawn_on_Opening_Day_of_the_Kabuki_Season_(IA_mma_perspective_picture_ukie_theater_district_at_dawn_on_opening_day_of_the_36669).jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

In Edo Japan, **uki-e** (“floating pictures”) borrowed Western perspective from imported prints from the 1730s and 1740s and used it for theatre streets and crowds in kimono — perspective re-meant, not copied. The twelfth-century Chinese scroll in Exercise 2 shows another system: space unrolled for a viewer who walks along it, with no single vanishing point.

Panofsky's own summary is precise: perspective mathematises visual space, but “it is an ordering, but an ordering of the visual phenomenon” [(Panofsky 1991, 71)](#ref-panofsky-1993). It orders what we see — not what things are.

### 4 · Three kinds of creative move

Margaret Boden gives a vocabulary for what changed in Florence. She calls the shared styles of a field **conceptual spaces** — “structured styles of thought” that are “normally picked up from one's own culture or peer group” [(Boden 2004, 4)](#ref-boden-2004). Her list explicitly includes fashions in couture and choreography. She also notes that dress values change across time and subcultures — the beehive hairdo and flared trousers of the 1960s [(Boden 2004, 10)](#ref-boden-2004).

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-conceptual-spaces" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/lesson-figures/visual-forger/vd-b-conceptual-spaces-6a1d7fd72bdf.svg' | relative_url }}" width="1600" height="900" alt="Course diagram after Margaret Boden: combinational, exploratory and transformational creative moves, each with an example from the history of pictorial space and one from fashion." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption><strong>Three kinds of creative move.</strong> Course diagram after Boden (2004) · Rubén Vega Balbás · CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Move | In pictorial space | In fashion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Combinational** — join two ideas that rarely meet | Uki-e: Western perspective + Japanese woodblock print | A tailoring code + a sportswear fabric |
| **Exploratory** — go further inside shared rules | 1300s painters testing boxes of space, step by step | Every skirt shape a pannier allows |
| **Transformational** — change a rule, so new things become thinkable | 1420s–1435: one vanishing point, measured on a plane | Kawakubo, 1997: padded bumps that redefine the silhouette |

**Studio question:** which kind of move is your image making? Most good student work is exploratory — and that is fine, if you can say which rule you are exploring.

### 5 · Perspective cuts both ways

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-holbein" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/5a45c628d9775b7c.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Holbein’s The Ambassadors, 1533: two richly dressed men beside instruments; a stretched skull across the floor becomes legible only from one oblique point." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Hans Holbein the Younger, <em>The Ambassadors</em>, 1533. National Gallery, London. Public domain · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_The_Ambassadors_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

Holbein hides a stretched skull — an **anamorphosis** — that reads only from one oblique spot beside the painting. The rule that orders space also makes the image depend on where you stand. Panofsky: “For perspective is by nature a two-edged sword” [(Panofsky 1991, 67)](#ref-panofsky-1993). It submits the picture to exact rules, and at the same time makes it depend on one individual's chosen point of view.

### 6 · Who holds the grid, who is measured?

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-durer-grid" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/0c2262302a302a30.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Dürer’s woodcut: a draughtsman looks through a gridded frame at a reclining, partly draped woman and copies her onto squared paper." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Albrecht Dürer, <em>Draughtsman Making a Perspective Drawing of a Reclining Woman</em>, woodcut from his treatise on measurement (1538 edition). The Metropolitan Museum of Art · CC0 · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Draughtsman_Making_a_Perspective_Drawing_of_a_Reclining_Woman_MET_DP816489.jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

A clothed man looks through a grid at a reclining, half-draped woman. Perspective is also a relation of **power** between the one who looks and the one looked at. Fashion images inherit the grid — lens, pose, crop — and the [dignity guide]({{ '/dignity/en/' | relative_url }}) asks who chooses them.

**Bridge to I.6.** No number of perfect views can fit a dress. A robe à la française of about 1765 takes its width from **panniers** underneath: views can agree while the under-structure stays invisible.

## Lab (portfolio) · 50′

> **Consent and care.** A garment on a chair is enough. Photograph a classmate only if they say yes. No AI is needed today.

### Exercise 1 · One eye, one step (25′)

1. Put your phone on a stack of books: this is your **station point**. Turn on the camera grid (your Alberti window).
2. Photograph a garment draped over a chair.
3. Move the phone **one step** sideways, same height. Shoot again.
4. Overlay or place the two shots side by side.

**Write one sentence:** what changed in the garment's volume when only the eye moved? Which fold or gap appeared that the first view hid?

### Exercise 2 · Same garment, two spaces (25′)

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-qingming" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/0934c6356384f204.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Detail of the Qingming scroll: a bridge crowded with people, boats and shops, drawn from a high, travelling viewpoint without one vanishing point." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Zhang Zeduan, <em>Along the River During the Qingming Festival</em> (detail), 12th century. Palace Museum, Beijing. Public domain · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Along_the_River_During_the_Qingming_Festival_(detail_of_original).jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

Draw the same garment twice, by hand or on a tablet:

1. **Central perspective.** One fixed station point, one vanishing point, a horizon at your eye height.
2. **Scroll space.** A long strip: draw the garment as you **walk past it**, letting front, side and back unroll without one vanishing point.

**Write one line under each drawing:** which symbolic form does it assume — whose eye, standing where?

**Portfolio entry:** the two shots (Ex 1) and the two drawings (Ex 2) with your sentences.

## Conclusion

Perspective is a **rule**, not the truth of vision: it assumes one still eye, orders what we see rather than what things are, and changes meaning between cultures. [Lecture C]({{ '/lessons/en/master-lectures/volume-depth-digital/' | relative_url }}) follows the rule into software — and into image generators that imitate it without any rule.
