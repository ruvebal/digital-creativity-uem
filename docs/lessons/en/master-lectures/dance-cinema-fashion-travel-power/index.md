## Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

1. Place a claim about influence on the **evidence ladder** (analogy → quotation → exchange → documented influence), and ask who is credited.
2. Tell **provocation** from **critique** using evidence — statements, reception, who wore it — not adjectives.
3. Explain why **a pose alone** cannot tell where a movement came from.
4. Transform a capture with **Photoshop or Firefly**, then **audit and declare** what the AI invented.

3. **Critical:** what does this image make *Spanish* — and who decided that?

## Masterclass · three ideas (40′)
### 6 · Movement travels with context

References travel: from stage to film, from film to runway, from one country to another. But a reference does not carry its history automatically. Use the ladder: **climb only as high as your evidence**.

<figure class="dcf-media dcf-diagram" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/master-lectures/dance-cinema-fashion/dcf-evidence-ladder.en.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Course diagram, an evidence ladder: 1 visual analogy, never enough alone; 2 quotation, a title or credit names the source; 3 exchange, a dated record of working together; 4 documented influence, testimony, dates and works line up. Examples: Kazuo Ohno's Admiring La Argentina (1977), with sources disagreeing on whether he saw her in 1926, 1927 or 1929; Forsythe and Miyake, Frankfurt 1991 or 1992, to Pleats Please 1993. Side panel: appropriation is a question of power, not a rung." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</figure>

**Case · Madrid → Tokyo.** Antonia Mercé, *La Argentina* (born Buenos Aires, 1890; died 1936), took Spanish dance onto international stages. The Japanese dancer Kazuo Ohno saw her perform as a young man, and some fifty years later made a solo titled after her, *Admiring La Argentina* (1977, directed by Tatsumi Hijikata). The title is a **quotation** you can check; the date Ohno saw her is not settled — the archives you can open give 1926, 1927 and 1929.

**Case · stage → product.** Issey Miyake's pleated costumes for William Forsythe's Frankfurt Ballet (*The Loss of Small Detail*, 1991; some accounts say 1992) are described as the prototype of the Pleats Please line (1993). That is an **exchange** with a product at the end — and still a claim to trace in dated records.

An American dancer presents herself as a Hindu goddess for Western audiences. **Ask:** who is named, paid or displaced when a practice moves into a new market? What record would separate influence, exchange, appropriation and mere visual analogy?

### 7 · Who is made strange?

Fashion has long pushed the body away from “natural” proportion; Henri Focillon wrote that “fashion invents an artificial humanity” (Focillon, quoted in [de Perthuis 2008, 169](#ref-de-perthuis-2008)). Fashion images can also refuse grace on purpose: Shinkle describes a model “tossed about wildly like a crash-test dummy”, outside the usual lexicon of poses [(Shinkle 2008, 222)](#ref-shinkle-2008).

<figure class="dcf-media" style="max-width: 520px" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/master-lectures/dance-cinema-fashion/rival-artistes-hb-1832-nypl.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="An 1832 lithograph caricature (HB Sketches no. 213): two men dressed as ballerinas, one in a blue tunic and plumed hat, the other in pink, compete in arabesque; speech bubbles above them." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption><strong>Costume as ridicule.</strong> <em>The Rival Artistes</em>, John Doyle (“HB”), HB Sketches no. 213, published by Thomas McLean, London, 17 July 1832: two public men are made strange by being dressed as ballet dancers. <em>Look:</em> who gets the tutu, and who is the joke for? Public domain · <a href="https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/4bf32a50-f8d0-0132-2235-58d385a7b928" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The New York Public Library</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

Spanish designers such as **David Delfín** and **Ana Locking** have been discussed in terms of provocation and the strange body; the Paris label **Matières Fécales** works with shock. *Abjection* is a theory about who is pushed out — **not** a synonym for “shocking image”. This session gives no verdict on them: in your own research, bring **evidence, not adjectives** — the designer's own statement, the press reception, who wore and who was made to look strange.

The newest case is close to this room. **Matières Fécales** (French for “fecal matter”), founded in Montreal in 2014 by Hannah Rose Dalton and Steven Raj Bhaskaran, built its name on disgust and transformation — and in 2026 dressed a Hollywood star for the Met Gala, whose theme that year was “Costume Art”.

<figcaption>Sarah Paulson at the 2026 Met Gala, in a Matières Fécales Fall/Winter 2026 gown as reported by <a href="https://www.out.com/celebs/celebrity-outfits-met-gala-2026" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Out.com</a>. <div class="dcf-watch" markdown="1">
**Open at source:** the label's resale shop on [Depop (@fecalmatter)](https://www.depop.com/fecalmatter/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. Depop does not allow its pages to be embedded, so it opens in a new tab. **Look:** how is “shock” photographed, described and priced when it is for sale?
</div>

**Ask:** a label named after disgust, on the most exclusive red carpet in fashion — critique, or a new kind of luxury? Who profits from the provocation?

Moving image can also open space: research on fashion films from 2010 to 2020 approaches queer representation through participation, experiences and discourses narrated through the format [(Navarro Gaviño 2022, 195)](#ref-navarro-gavino-2022).

**Ask:** Does this provocation contest a norm — or turn excluded bodies into spectacle? Whose account, and which reception evidence, could tell the difference?

### 8 · Butoh has histories, not one look

*Butō* began in post-war Japan (Tatsumi Hijikata, Kazuo Ohno) and spread in many directions, including a long reception in Argentina. Its best-known images — white body paint, slow distortion — are the easiest thing to copy and the least informative. **A pose alone cannot tell us where a movement came from.** Notice that Ohno's most famous solo, from idea 6, is a tribute to a Spanish dancer: the history crosses the look.

<figure class="dcf-media" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/master-lectures/dance-cinema-fashion/sankai-juku-kagemi-2006.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Dark stage: three dancers with shaved, whitened heads and bodies crouch in loincloths at the front, hands raised near their faces; behind them three whitened figures in long earth-coloured dresses stand with hands lifted to their eyes." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>Sankai Juku, <em>Kagemi: Beyond the Metaphor of Mirrors</em>, Festival Internacional Cervantino, Guanajuato (Mexico), October 2006. Photograph Carlos de las Piedras, CC BY 2.0 · <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sankai_Juku.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Wikimedia Commons</a>. A second-generation butoh company (founded by Ushio Amagatsu, 1975) on a Mexican stage: another reception history, not a timeless “look”.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is also what happens when an image generator learns from scraped pictures. Mejias and Couldry call large-scale data capture “extensive acts of appropriation” and argue that “the ‘cool’ factor of ‘generative AI’ is basically a device to distract from this” [(Mejias and Couldry 2024, introduction)](#ref-mejias-couldry-2024). You will test this in the Lab audit.

**Ask:** Who can name a tradition, who earns from its circulation, and when does exoticisation erase context?

## Lab (portfolio) · 50′

Two exercises. Work on a capture from Part 1 or Part 2 — or shoot a new one in two minutes. Everything you make goes to your portfolio index / Canvas with a short note.

<div class="dcf-guard" markdown="1">
**Consent and care (non-negotiable).** Photograph a classmate only if they say yes, and show them the result. Faces are optional — hands, hems, shadows and backs are enough. **Never upload a classmate's image to Firefly or any cloud AI without their explicit consent**; when in doubt, use your own body, your garment, or an object. Delete on request. Student work is authorial property: nothing is exhibited without a separate permission.
</div>

### Exercise 1 · Transform with Photoshop or Firefly (25′)

Choose **one** transform. Keep **Content Credentials** on when you export.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T2 · Reframe for cinema (Generative Expand).** Take a vertical C1/C3 still. Photoshop **Crop tool → Fill: Generative Expand** to 16:9. Then draw a red outline around every pixel the AI invented. Question: who decided what is off-frame — and what did it assume about the space, the audience, the body?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T3 · Extend the garment (Generative Fill).** Lasso around the sleeve or hem and prompt one of:
`pleated silk unfolding into wide wings, theatrical side light, motion blur`
`rigid painted geometric shell around the arm, disc and cone forms, stage spotlight`
Generate three variants. Which one could a body actually move in? Annotate why.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T4 · Firefly with your photo as structure.** Firefly **Text to image**, upload your C-photo as **Composition reference** (strength high). Prompt template:
`[garment + material] in motion, [light: side / back / coloured], full figure, plain wall, documentary photograph`
Keep your pose; change only the garment. Compare: what did the model keep of your movement, and what did it smooth away?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T5 · Animate a still (Firefly video · I.8 bridge).** Firefly **Generate video → Image to video** from your best C-still, about 5 s:
`the fabric keeps swirling, camera static, same lighting`
Put it side by side with your real Slo-mo from C1. What physics did the model invent for the cloth?
</div>

### Exercise 2 · Audit what the machine assumes (25′)

Everyone who used a generative step does this audit. It is the evidence ladder applied to a machine.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**AUDIT (mandatory with any generative step).** In Firefly, generate four images for `butoh dancer` **or** `flamenco dancer`. List what the model assumes: skin, make-up, gender, age, setting, costume. Then write **one sentence** using the evidence ladder: *which rung can a generated image reach?* (Hint: it can only resemble — rung 1.)
</div>

Add one more sentence: which idea of this part — travel, strangeness or butoh — does your audit test, and what did you find?

**Portfolio entry:** one transform (Ex 1) + your audit sentences (Ex 2) + a two-line AI declaration — tool, what it contributed, what you checked — following the [course AI declaration]({{ '/ai-declaration/' | relative_url }}). Generative credits and features depend on your Adobe plan; if a feature is unavailable, stack layers by hand (Part 1, Exercise 2) and audit a classmate's generated images with their permission.

## Conclusion

Climb **only as high as your evidence** — even famous influence stories have disputed dates. Ask **who is credited, paid or displaced** when a movement travels. The AI audit is the same habit applied to machines: a generated image can resemble a tradition; it cannot document one.
