## Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

1. Name what a garment does to movement — **drape, extension, tension or reorganisation** — and defend it with one frame.
2. Explain how **frame size and cutting** decide what a film lets us see of a dressed body.
3. Say who is credited when a costume, a star or a camera shapes a dance.
4. Build a **constraint costume**, and film one phrase as **one take and as three cuts**.

## Analysis · one costume drawing (15′)

Read one costume drawing with the [eight-step card]({{ '/lessons/en/master-lectures/fashion-image-analysis/' | relative_url }}#quick-card-printable). Work in pairs, three minutes per block.

1. **Describe:** dot, line, plane — the rings, the disc, the stripes. Where is the body?
2. **Origin / production:** a painter designs a dancer on paper before anyone can move in it. What will the dancer have to do to wear it?
3. **Critical:** who is the author of the dance this costume allows — the designer or the dancer?

### 5 · Cinema frames the moving body

A camera does not simply record a dance. It chooses a frame, a distance and a cut. Even so, cameras “in most cases still cling to and follow concrete body movements”, and slow motion and changing perspectives can dramatise what ordinary perception misses [(Pfeiffer 2009, 198)](#ref-pfeiffer-2009).

<figure class="dcf-media dcf-diagram" markdown="0">

Two timelines of the same 30 seconds: one take, or six cuts. Questions: are the feet and the hem in frame; who chooses the cut; what does the close-up sell, the dress or the star?" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</figure>

Studios understood this. Hollywood used fashion photography to give stars a signature look and presented the star as a fashion reference [(Sill 2008, 130)](#ref-sill-2008) — yet “it's the public who makes the star” [(Sill 2008, 127)](#ref-sill-2008).

In 1934 Walter Benjamin argued that the stage had a choice when film and radio arrived — compete, or learn. The position worth taking is that of a theatre that does the second:

> "Not so the position of a theatre which, instead of competing against the newer means of communication, tries to apply them and to learn from them - in short, to enter into a dialogue with them."  
> — [(Benjamin 1998, 99)](#ref-benjamin-1998)

Chomón's films, below, are that dialogue seen from cinema's side; your phone camera is this session's version of it.

**A Spanish pioneer frames the serpentine.** Segundo de Chomón (Teruel 1871 – Paris 1929) coloured films by hand and by stencil in Barcelona and Paris, and turned the serpentine into special effects — the dance as animation.

## Lab (portfolio) · 50′

Two exercises with your phone. Everything you make goes to your portfolio index / Canvas with a short note.

<div class="dcf-guard" markdown="1">

Faces are optional — hands, hems, shadows and backs are enough. **Never upload a classmate's image to Firefly or any cloud AI without their explicit consent**; when in doubt, use your own body, your garment, or an object. Delete on request. Student work is authorial property: nothing is exhibited without a separate permission.

### Exercise 1 · Constraint costume (25′)

Build a costume that decides the step, then film what it forces.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C4 · Constraint costume (after Schlemmer).** In five minutes, build one rigid extension — paper cone, cardboard disc, coat hanger, tape — on an arm, the head or the hem. Film the movement it **forces**. One sentence: what step became impossible, and what new step appeared?
</div>

### Where this goes next

Fashion film is the heir of advertising, cinema, short film, video clips and video art, and a form of branded content [(Díaz Soloaga and García Guerrero 2016, 49)](#ref-diaz-soloaga-2016). Interactive stages go further: in Chunky Move's *Glow*, an overhead camera tracks the dancer so that a projector maps light and graphics precisely onto the body's coordinates [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 47)](#ref-eckersall-2017) — the body becomes “productive of the specific shape of its immediate digital environment” [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 48)](#ref-eckersall-2017). Projection always raises “questions about who and what is visible” and “who is looking” [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 25)](#ref-eckersall-2017).
