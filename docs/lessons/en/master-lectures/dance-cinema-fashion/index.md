> **A garment changes what a moving body can show and do. A camera and an edit change what we can see of both.**

This is a **Master Lecture** for Digital Creativity I. Its principal home is the official content *Fuentes y referencias compositivas en el diseño de moda* (I.7): dance, film and stage costume are compositional sources for fashion. The animation exercise at the end (Ex 2 · T5) touches *Creación de imágenes digitales animadas* (I.8). It is **not** a new unit, deliverable or grade: what you make goes to your portfolio as Lab evidence.

**Bring:** your phone (charged, with free space), a garment that moves (scarf, shirt, coat, skirt), and your Adobe Creative Cloud login.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

1. Name what a garment does to movement — **drape, extension, tension or reorganisation** — and point to the frame of film that shows it.
2. Explain how **frame size and cutting** decide what a film lets us see of a dressed body.
3. Place a claim about influence on the **evidence ladder** (analogy → quotation → exchange → documented influence), and ask who is credited.
4. Capture movement with your **phone camera**, transform it with **Photoshop or Firefly**, and **audit and declare** what the AI invented.

3. **Critical:** who leans and who holds? What does the photograph freeze that the dance would never stop on?

## Masterclass · six ideas (40′)

### 1 · The moving, dressed body

A garment is not a costume until it moves. Looking at movement is also bodily: when we look at a represented body, we “map these postures and gestures onto our own body, feeling them in our own skin and bones” [(Shinkle 2008, 222)](#ref-shinkle-2008).

Duncan's “Greek” tunic and Fortuny's pleated column both borrow an idea of antiquity. Very little film of Duncan dancing is known; her dance reaches us mostly through photographs like Genthe's — a still medium for a moving art.

**Ask:** Which “classical” body becomes the universal ideal here — and whose bodies and histories are left out of that ideal?

### 2 · Costume makes a choreography

Some costumes follow the body; others become the dance. Loïe Fuller patented a “garment for dancers” in 1894: a wide skirt hung from the head, with light wands of aluminium or bamboo that let the dancer throw the cloth into waves and wings — open the [patent itself](https://patents.google.com/patent/US518347A/en){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} as a primary record.

Then something happened that matters to anyone studying references: **the serpentine dance was copied everywhere, and most early films labelled “Loïe Fuller” show other dancers.** Watch three of them as evidence, not as illustration.

Kandinsky's Bauhaus notebook gives you a looking tool for these films: “The entire body of the dancer, right down to his finger tips, is at every moment an uninterrupted composition of lines” — he credits the observation to the dancer Gret Palucca [(Kandinsky 2012, “Line”)](#ref-kandinsky-2012).

At the opposite pole, Oskar Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet* (premiere, Stuttgart, 30 September 1922) built **rigid, geometric figures** that decide how the body can step and turn.

**Ask:** Who made these garments, who could move in them, and who gets the authorship credit — dancer, designer, maker, or the person who filed the patent?

### 3 · Cinema frames the moving body

A camera does not simply record a dance. It chooses a frame, a distance and a cut. Even so, cameras “in most cases still cling to and follow concrete body movements”, and slow motion and changing perspectives can dramatise what ordinary perception misses [(Pfeiffer 2009, 198)](#ref-pfeiffer-2009).

### 4 · Movement travels with context

References travel: from stage to film, from film to runway, from one country to another. But a reference does not carry its history automatically. Use the ladder: **climb only as high as your evidence**.

**Case · Madrid → Tokyo.** Antonia Mercé, *La Argentina* (born Buenos Aires, 1890; died 1936), took Spanish dance onto international stages. The Japanese dancer Kazuo Ohno saw her perform as a young man, and some fifty years later made a solo titled after her, *Admiring La Argentina* (1977, directed by Tatsumi Hijikata). The title is a **quotation** you can check; the date Ohno saw her is not settled — the archives you can open give 1926, 1927 and 1929.

**Case · stage → product.** Issey Miyake's pleated costumes for William Forsythe's Frankfurt Ballet (*The Loss of Small Detail*, 1991; some accounts say 1992) are described as the prototype of the Pleats Please line (1993). That is an **exchange** with a product at the end — and still a claim to trace in dated records.

An American dancer presents herself as a Hindu goddess for Western audiences. **Ask:** who is named, paid or displaced when a practice moves into a new market? What record would separate influence, exchange, appropriation and mere visual analogy?

### 5 · Who is made strange?

Fashion has long pushed the body away from “natural” proportion; Henri Focillon wrote that “fashion invents an artificial humanity” (Focillon, quoted in [de Perthuis 2008, 169](#ref-de-perthuis-2008)). Fashion images can also refuse grace on purpose: Shinkle describes a model “tossed about wildly like a crash-test dummy”, outside the usual lexicon of poses [(Shinkle 2008, 222)](#ref-shinkle-2008).

Spanish designers such as **David Delfín** and **Ana Locking** have been discussed in terms of provocation and the strange body; the Paris label **Matières Fécales** works with shock. *Abjection* is a theory about who is pushed out — **not** a synonym for “shocking image”. This session gives no verdict on them: in your own research, bring **evidence, not adjectives** — the designer's own statement, the press reception, who wore and who was made to look strange.

Moving image can also open space: research on fashion films from 2010 to 2020 approaches queer representation through participation, experiences and discourses narrated through the format [(Navarro Gaviño 2022, 195)](#ref-navarro-gavino-2022).

**Ask:** Does this provocation contest a norm — or turn excluded bodies into spectacle? Whose account, and which reception evidence, could tell the difference?

### 6 · Butoh has histories, not one look

*Butō* began in post-war Japan (Tatsumi Hijikata, Kazuo Ohno) and spread in many directions, including a long reception in Argentina. Its best-known images — white body paint, slow distortion — are the easiest thing to copy and the least informative. **A pose alone cannot tell us where a movement came from.** Notice that Ohno's most famous solo, from idea 4, is a tribute to a Spanish dancer: the history crosses the look.

This is also what happens when an image generator learns from scraped pictures. Mejias and Couldry call large-scale data capture “extensive acts of appropriation” and argue that “the ‘cool’ factor of ‘generative AI’ is basically a device to distract from this” [(Mejias and Couldry 2024, introduction)](#ref-mejias-couldry-2024). You will test this in the Lab audit.

**Ask:** Who can name a tradition, who earns from its circulation, and when does exoticisation erase context?

Two exercises. Each offers a **menu of prompts** — choose; do not do them all. Everything you make goes to your portfolio index / Canvas with a short note.

Faces are optional — hands, hems, shadows and backs are enough. **Never upload a classmate's image to Firefly or any cloud AI without their explicit consent**; when in doubt, use your own body, your garment, or an object. Delete on request. Student work is authorial property: nothing is exhibited without a separate permission.

### Exercise 1 · Capture with the phone camera (25′)

Choose **two** prompts. Plain wall, side light (a window, or a classmate's phone torch). Shoot many; keep few.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C1 · Serpentine study (after Fuller).** Spin with a scarf, shirt or coat held out on your arms or on two pens as “wands”. Film 5 seconds in **Slo-mo** (iPhone) or **Slow motion** (Android). Darken the corner and colour the light: put a full-screen colour on a second phone and use it as a gel.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C2 · Chronophotograph (after Muybridge).** One turn, **burst mode** (hold the shutter, or swipe it left on iPhone), about 12 frames, phone fixed against a bag or wall. You need these frames for Ex 2 · T1.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C3 · Same dance, different film (after Astaire).** One 8-count phrase, twice: **A** one take, full figure, feet and hem in frame; **B** three short close-ups (hem, hands, face) cut together in your phone's editor. Compare with the framing diagram: what does B sell?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C4 · Constraint costume (after Schlemmer).** In five minutes, build one rigid extension — paper cone, cardboard disc, coat hanger, tape — on an arm, the head or the hem. Film the movement it **forces**. One sentence: what step became impossible, and what new step appeared?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C5 · The dancer draws lines (after Kandinsky).** Long exposure: iPhone **Live Photo → Long Exposure** effect, or Android **Night / Pro** mode at 1–2 s. Move a garment with a phone torch tucked into it. The light becomes the line the body draws.
</div>

### Exercise 2 · Transform with Photoshop or Firefly — and audit (25′)

Choose **one** transform. The **audit is mandatory** for everyone who uses generative AI. Keep **Content Credentials** on when you export.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T1 · One image of time (no AI).** Photoshop: *File → Scripts → Load Files into Stack* with six C2 frames. Set every layer to **Lighten** (dark wall) or 25% opacity. Mask the tripod-wobble. Result: the garment's path in one image.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T2 · Reframe for cinema (Generative Expand).** Take a vertical C1/C3 still. Photoshop **Crop tool → Fill: Generative Expand** to 16:9. Then draw a red outline around every pixel the AI invented. Question: who decided what is off-frame — and what did it assume about the space, the audience, the body?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T3 · Extend the garment (Generative Fill).** Lasso around the sleeve or hem and prompt one of:
`pleated silk unfolding into wide wings, theatrical side light, motion blur`
`rigid painted geometric shell around the arm, disc and cone forms, stage spotlight`
Generate three variants. Which one could a body actually move in? Annotate why.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T4 · Firefly with your photo as structure.** Firefly **Text to image**, upload your C-photo as **Composition reference** (strength high). Prompt template:
`[garment + material] in motion, [light: side / back / coloured], full figure, plain wall, documentary photograph`
Keep your pose; change only the garment. Compare: what did the model keep of your movement, and what did it smooth away?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T5 · Animate a still (Firefly video · I.8 bridge).** Firefly **Generate video → Image to video** from your best C-still, about 5 s:
`the fabric keeps swirling, camera static, same lighting`
Put it side by side with your real Slo-mo from C1. What physics did the model invent for the cloth?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**AUDIT (mandatory with any generative step).** In Firefly, generate four images for `butoh dancer` **or** `flamenco dancer`. List what the model assumes: skin, make-up, gender, age, setting, costume. Then write **one sentence** using the evidence ladder: *which rung can a generated image reach?* (Hint: it can only resemble — rung 1.)
</div>

**Portfolio entry:** one capture (Ex 1) + one transform (Ex 2) + your audit sentence + a two-line AI declaration — tool, what it contributed, what you checked — following the [course AI declaration]({{ '/ai-declaration/' | relative_url }}). Generative credits and features depend on your Adobe plan; if a feature is unavailable, do T1.

### Where this goes next

Fashion film is the heir of advertising, cinema, short film, video clips and video art, and a form of branded content [(Díaz Soloaga and García Guerrero 2016, 49)](#ref-diaz-soloaga-2016). Interactive stages go further: in Chunky Move's *Glow*, an overhead camera tracks the dancer so that a projector maps light and graphics precisely onto the body's coordinates [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 47)](#ref-eckersall-2017) — the body becomes “productive of the specific shape of its immediate digital environment” [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 48)](#ref-eckersall-2017). Projection always raises “questions about who and what is visible” and “who is looking” [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 25)](#ref-eckersall-2017).

## Conclusion

Three habits leave this room with you. Watch **what the cloth does after the body moves**. Count **the cuts** before you praise the dance. And climb **only as high as your evidence** — even famous influence stories have disputed dates. The AI audit is the same habit applied to machines: a generated image can resemble a tradition; it cannot document one.
