## Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

1. Name what a garment does to movement — **drape, extension, tension or reorganisation** — and point to the frame of film that shows it.
2. Treat an old film as **evidence**: who is really dancing, who made the copy, which date is right.
3. Explain how **frame size and cutting** decide what a film lets us see of a dressed body.
4. Capture movement with your **phone camera** and turn a burst into **one image of time**.

## Masterclass · three ideas (40′)

### 1 · The moving, dressed body

A garment is not a costume until it moves. Looking at movement is also bodily: when we look at a represented body, we “map these postures and gestures onto our own body, feeling them in our own skin and bones” [(Shinkle 2008, 222)](#ref-shinkle-2008).

The movement analyst Rudolf Laban put the same idea from the dancer's side:

> «Cada fase del movimiento, cada pequeña transferencia de peso, cada solitario gesto de una parte del cuerpo, revela algún rasgo de nuestra vida interior».  
> — [(Laban 1987, 39)](#ref-laban-1987) · *Course translation:* “Every phase of movement, every small transfer of weight, every single gesture of any part of the body reveals some feature of our inner life.”

Duncan's “Greek” tunic and Fortuny's pleated column both borrow an idea of antiquity. Very little film of Duncan dancing is known; her dance reaches us mostly through photographs like Genthe's — a still medium for a moving art.

**Ask:** Which “classical” body becomes the universal ideal here — and whose bodies and histories are left out of that ideal?

### 2 · Costume makes a choreography

Some costumes follow the body; others become the dance. Loïe Fuller patented a “garment for dancers” in 1894: a wide skirt hung from the head, with light wands of aluminium or bamboo that let the dancer throw the cloth into waves and wings — open the [patent itself](https://patents.google.com/patent/US518347A/en){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} as a primary record.

Then something happened that matters to anyone studying references: **the serpentine dance was copied everywhere, and most early films labelled “Loïe Fuller” show other dancers.** Watch three of them as evidence, not as illustration.

Kandinsky's Bauhaus notebook gives you a looking tool for these films: “The entire body of the dancer, right down to his finger tips, is at every moment an uninterrupted composition of lines” — he credits the observation to the dancer Gret Palucca [(Kandinsky 2012, “Line”)](#ref-kandinsky-2012).

At the opposite pole, Oskar Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet* (premiere, Stuttgart, 30 September 1922) built **rigid, geometric figures** that decide how the body can step and turn.

Schlemmer did not start from geometry, though. His diaries start from the plain fact of a body on stage:

> «Partimos de la situación corporal, de la existencia, del estar de pié, de caminar y, luego, en último lugar, del saltar y del danzar. Puesto que dar un paso es un acontecimiento grandioso, mientras que levantar una mano, mover un dedo, no lo son menos».  
> — [(Schlemmer 1987, 112)](#ref-schlemmer-1987) · *Course translation:* “We start from the bodily situation, from existence, from standing, from walking, and only last of all from leaping and dancing. For taking a step is a grand event — and raising a hand or moving a finger no less.”

### 3 · Cinema frames the moving body

A camera does not simply record a dance. It chooses a frame, a distance and a cut. Even so, cameras “in most cases still cling to and follow concrete body movements”, and slow motion and changing perspectives can dramatise what ordinary perception misses [(Pfeiffer 2009, 198)](#ref-pfeiffer-2009).

Studios understood this. Hollywood used fashion photography to give stars a signature look and presented the star as a fashion reference [(Sill 2008, 130)](#ref-sill-2008) — yet “it's the public who makes the star” [(Sill 2008, 127)](#ref-sill-2008).

In 1934 Walter Benjamin argued that the stage had a choice when film and radio arrived — compete, or learn. The position worth taking is that of a theatre that does the second:

> "Not so the position of a theatre which, instead of competing against the newer means of communication, tries to apply them and to learn from them - in short, to enter into a dialogue with them."  
> — [(Benjamin 1998, 99)](#ref-benjamin-1998)

Chomón's films, below, are that dialogue seen from cinema's side; your phone camera is this session's version of it; Firefly comes in Part 2.

**A Spanish pioneer frames the serpentine.** Segundo de Chomón (Teruel 1871 – Paris 1929) coloured films by hand and by stencil in Barcelona and Paris, and turned the serpentine into special effects — the dance as animation.

## Lab (portfolio) · 50′

Two exercises. Each offers a **menu of prompts** — choose; do not do them all. Everything you make goes to your portfolio index / Canvas with a short note.

<div class="dcf-guard" markdown="1">
**Consent and care (non-negotiable).** Photograph a classmate only if they say yes, and show them the result. Faces are optional — hands, hems, shadows and backs are enough. **Never upload a classmate's image to Firefly or any cloud AI without their explicit consent**; when in doubt, use your own body, your garment, or an object. Delete on request. Student work is authorial property: nothing is exhibited without a separate permission.
</div>

</figure>

### Exercise 1 · Capture with the phone camera (25′)

Choose **two** prompts. Plain wall, side light (a window, or a classmate's phone torch). Shoot many; keep few.

While you shoot, watch with Laban's four motion factors: «Peso, espacio, tiempo, y flujo» — weight, space, time and flow [(Laban 1987, 28)](#ref-laban-1987). Name which one your garment changes most.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C1 · Serpentine study (after Fuller).** Spin with a scarf, shirt or coat held out on your arms or on two pens as “wands”. Film 5 seconds in **Slo-mo** (iPhone) or **Slow motion** (Android). Darken the corner and colour the light: put a full-screen colour on a second phone and use it as a gel.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C2 · Chronophotograph (after Muybridge).** One turn, **burst mode** (hold the shutter, or swipe it left on iPhone), about 12 frames, phone fixed against a bag or wall. You need these frames for Ex 2 · T1.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C3 · Same dance, different film (after Astaire).** One 8-count phrase, twice: **A** one take, full figure, feet and hem in frame; **B** three short close-ups (hem, hands, face) cut together in your phone's editor. Compare with the framing diagram: what does B sell?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C4 · Constraint costume (after Schlemmer).** In five minutes, build one rigid extension — paper cone, cardboard disc, coat hanger, tape — on an arm, the head or the hem. Film the movement it **forces**. One sentence: what step became impossible, and what new step appeared?
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C5 · The dancer draws lines (after Kandinsky).** Long exposure: iPhone **Live Photo → Long Exposure** effect, or Android **Night / Pro** mode at 1–2 s. Move a garment with a phone torch tucked into it. The light becomes the line the body draws.
</div>

### Exercise 2 · One image of time, read with the movement card (25′)

Turn your burst into a single image, then read it as if it were a film still.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T1 · One image of time (no AI).** Photoshop: *File → Scripts → Load Files into Stack* with six C2 frames. Set every layer to **Lighten** (dark wall) or 25% opacity. Mask the tripod-wobble. Result: the garment's path in one image.
</div>

Then fill the **movement card** below for your own image: what the garment does, the frame, what the frame hides, and who would be credited.

**Portfolio entry:** your best capture (Ex 1) + your image of time (Ex 2) + the filled movement card. Today needs no AI; if you used any tool that generates content, declare it following the [course AI declaration]({{ '/ai-declaration/' | relative_url }}).

### Where this goes next

Fashion film is the heir of advertising, cinema, short film, video clips and video art, and a form of branded content [(Díaz Soloaga and García Guerrero 2016, 49)](#ref-diaz-soloaga-2016). Interactive stages go further: in Chunky Move's *Glow*, an overhead camera tracks the dancer so that a projector maps light and graphics precisely onto the body's coordinates [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 47)](#ref-eckersall-2017) — the body becomes “productive of the specific shape of its immediate digital environment” [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 48)](#ref-eckersall-2017). Projection always raises “questions about who and what is visible” and “who is looking” [(Eckersall, Grehan, and Scheer 2017, 25)](#ref-eckersall-2017).
