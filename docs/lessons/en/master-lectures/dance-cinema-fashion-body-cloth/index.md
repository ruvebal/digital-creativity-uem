## Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

1. Say what a garment does **after** the body moves, and point to it in a photograph or film.
2. Treat an old film as **evidence**: who is really dancing, who made the copy, which date is right.
3. Read a dancing body as **lines**, and a single step as a designed event.
4. Capture movement with your **phone camera** and turn a burst into **one image of time**.

## Masterclass · three ideas (40′)
### 1 · The moving, dressed body

A garment is not a costume until it moves. Looking at movement is also bodily: when we look at a represented body, we “map these postures and gestures onto our own body, feeling them in our own skin and bones” [(Shinkle 2008, 222)](#ref-shinkle-2008).

The movement analyst Rudolf Laban put the same idea from the dancer's side:

> «Cada fase del movimiento, cada pequeña transferencia de peso, cada solitario gesto de una parte del cuerpo, revela algún rasgo de nuestra vida interior».  
> — [(Laban 1987, 39)](#ref-laban-1987) · *Course translation:* “Every phase of movement, every small transfer of weight, every single gesture of any part of the body reveals some feature of our inner life.”

Duncan's “Greek” tunic and Fortuny's pleated column both borrow an idea of antiquity. Very little film of Duncan dancing is known; her dance reaches us mostly through photographs like Genthe's — a still medium for a moving art.

**Ask:** Which “classical” body becomes the universal ideal here — and whose bodies and histories are left out of that ideal?

### 2 · Cloth that flies

Some costumes follow the body; others become the dance. Loïe Fuller patented a “garment for dancers” in 1894: a wide skirt hung from the head, with light wands of aluminium or bamboo that let the dancer throw the cloth into waves and wings — open the [patent itself](https://patents.google.com/patent/US518347A/en){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} as a primary record.

Kandinsky's Bauhaus notebook gives you a looking tool for these films: “The entire body of the dancer, right down to his finger tips, is at every moment an uninterrupted composition of lines” — he credits the observation to the dancer Gret Palucca [(Kandinsky 2012, “Line”)](#ref-kandinsky-2012).

**Ask:** Who made these garments, who could move in them, and who gets the authorship credit — dancer, designer, maker, or the person who filed the patent?

### 3 · Start from one step

Oskar Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet* premiered in Stuttgart on 30 September 1922. Its costumes look like pure geometry, but Schlemmer did not start from geometry. His diaries start from the plain fact of a body on stage:

> «Partimos de la situación corporal, de la existencia, del estar de pié, de caminar y, luego, en último lugar, del saltar y del danzar. Puesto que dar un paso es un acontecimiento grandioso, mientras que levantar una mano, mover un dedo, no lo son menos».  
> — [(Schlemmer 1987, 112)](#ref-schlemmer-1987) · *Course translation:* “We start from the bodily situation, from existence, from standing, from walking, and only last of all from leaping and dancing. For taking a step is a grand event — and raising a hand or moving a finger no less.”

**Ask:** What does a single step need from a garment — and what can a garment stop it from doing?

## Lab (portfolio) · 50′

Two exercises with your phone. Everything you make goes to your portfolio index / Canvas with a short note.

<div class="dcf-guard" markdown="1">
**Consent and care (non-negotiable).** Photograph a classmate only if they say yes, and show them the result. Faces are optional — hands, hems, shadows and backs are enough. **Never upload a classmate's image to Firefly or any cloud AI without their explicit consent**; when in doubt, use your own body, your garment, or an object. Delete on request. Student work is authorial property: nothing is exhibited without a separate permission.
</div>

### Exercise 1 · Serpentine and light (25′)

Do one or both. Plain wall, side light (a window, or a classmate's phone torch). Shoot many; keep few.

While you shoot, watch with Laban's four motion factors: «Peso, espacio, tiempo, y flujo» — weight, space, time and flow [(Laban 1987, 28)](#ref-laban-1987). Name which one your garment changes most.

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C1 · Serpentine study (after Fuller).** Spin with a scarf, shirt or coat held out on your arms or on two pens as “wands”. Film 5 seconds in **Slo-mo** (iPhone) or **Slow motion** (Android). Darken the corner and colour the light: put a full-screen colour on a second phone and use it as a gel.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C5 · The dancer draws lines (after Kandinsky).** Long exposure: iPhone **Live Photo → Long Exposure** effect, or Android **Night / Pro** mode at 1–2 s. Move a garment with a phone torch tucked into it. The light becomes the line the body draws.
</div>

### Exercise 2 · One image of time (25′)

Shoot a burst of one turn, then stack it into a single image.

<figure class="dcf-media dcf-diagram" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/master-lectures/dance-cinema-fashion/dcf-onion-skin.en.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Course diagram of the Lab method: 1 shoot a burst of about twelve frames of a turn; 2 keep six; 3 stack them as Photoshop layers at 25 percent opacity or Lighten blend; 4 one image shows how the garment travelled through time." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</figure>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**C2 · Chronophotograph (after Muybridge).** One turn, **burst mode** (hold the shutter, or swipe it left on iPhone), about 12 frames, phone fixed against a bag or wall. You need these frames for Ex 2 · T1.
</div>

<div class="dcf-prompt" markdown="1">
**T1 · One image of time (no AI).** Photoshop: *File → Scripts → Load Files into Stack* with six C2 frames. Set every layer to **Lighten** (dark wall) or 25% opacity. Mask the tripod-wobble. Result: the garment's path in one image.
</div>
