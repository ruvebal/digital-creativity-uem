## Learning objectives

- **Explain, with a real citation, why sourcing a compositional reference is not a neutral act of collection.**
- **Treat the moodboard as a research object** that answers a stated question — not as collage décor.
- **Keep a reference ledger** for every tile: source, reason for selection, cultural position, transformation, and what you deliberately did not copy.
- **Refuse a Euro-only default board:** include at least one tile whose source decentralises white / Global North fashion historiography, with ledger fields completed.
- **Distinguish inspiration, quotation, adaptation, and infringement** as studio decisions — and “a reference that informs” from “a reference that is copied.”

## Analysis

### Critical perspective

What a search surfaces already carries a representational politics — before you choose a tile. Campinho, Cardim da Silva, Dantas Soares, and Amparo-Santos studied Google Images searches for “fat body” and “obese body.” They found contrasting patterns of empowerment and medicalised fragmentation that shape collective perceptions of bodies [(Campinho 2025, 2)](#ref-campinho-2025).

Whose context, agency, and right not to be reused are missing when a source becomes a moodboard tile? Name one pattern your own sourcing surfaced before you chose anything.

### The debate prompt

**If a mood-board search reproduces a biased pattern before you've chosen anything, whose responsibility is the bias — the search engine's, the archive's, or the student's for not noticing?** No source in this unit settles it; naming Campinho's finding makes the question unavoidable once you know to look.

## Masterclass

**Claim:** sourcing a compositional reference is not a neutral act of collection — *what a search surfaces* already carries a representational politics, before a student has chosen which image to reference at all.

> The images of the fat body show an expression of empowerment. On the other hand, fragmented bodies are linked to a cultural production that associates the fat, so-called obese, body with disease.
> — [(Campinho 2025, 2)](#ref-campinho-2025)

**What this supports, and what it does not.** Campinho et al. (2025, 2) grounds the *ethics of the images students will encounter while sourcing* — a real demonstration that a search's own results carry a representational pattern before any creative choice. It does **not** ground a validated *teaching method for compositional-reference-sourcing* itself — no source here studies how to teach mood-boarding, visual research, or reference-attribution practice as a classroom sequence. Tool docs ground operations only — label `[PLATFORM]`, never research.

### Critical perspective · Non-Eurocentric sourcing

Fashion archives often reflect and perpetuate colonial legacies in curation, classification, and access — so “what appears in search” is already a historiography [(Rodriguez Schon and Valle-Noronha 2025, 1)](#ref-rodriguez-schon-2025). Decolonising that space means more than diversifying collections: taxonomies, temporalities, and display practices that make fashion knowable must be interrogated [(Rodriguez Schon and Valle-Noronha 2025, 2)](#ref-rodriguez-schon-2025). Platforms such as the Fashion and Race Database are documented cases that centre minority-group histories against white / Global North accounts [(Rodriguez Schon and Valle-Noronha 2025, 8)](#ref-rodriguez-schon-2025). Cheang and Suterwalla argue that the load-bearing move is a shift in consciousness and teacher positionality — not a model syllabus of “diverse tiles” pasted onto an intact European canon [(Cheang and Suterwalla 2020, 1)](#ref-cheang-suterwalla-2020).

**Course rule:** a board that only reproduces European / US fashion photography without naming that limit fails the cultural-position field — even if every tile is credited.

**Open living prompt (keep):** How can a reference remain useful while preserving the source's context, agency, and right not to be reused — especially when the source is racialised, indigenous, or diasporic and the search surface was not built for that history?

### 1 · Moodboard as research (not collage décor)

A board should answer a research question you state in one sentence before you collect tiles. Select images that argue; reject pretty-but-mute tiles. Annotate why each tile earns its place. Write a short synthesis that the board alone cannot say. This is course practice drawn from the field’s own treatment of moodboards as research tools; the primary articles for that strand are not yet cleared for public author-date citation here — they stay as an honest gap in the Editorial note, not as invented proof.

**Course nail (assessable):** every board needs **source · reason for selection · cultural position · transformation · what you deliberately did not copy**.

### 2 · Reference ledger (keep this visible)

| Field | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| Source | Creator, title/desc, date, URL or repository, licence/permission |
| Reason | Why this fragment answers the research question |
| Cultural position | Whose body, class, geography, or archive is speaking — and who is absent |
| Transformation | What changes when it enters your composition (verb + result) |
| Refusal | What you will not copy (face, period accessory, whole silhouette, brand mark…) |

No anonymous screenshots. A peer must be able to reconstruct two sources from your ledger.

Photographic fashion images are themselves a field of practices with commercial and cultural force [(Shinkle 2008, 15)](#ref-shinkle-2008) — treat every tile as a decision inside that field, not a free texture pack.

### 3 · Inspiration · quotation · adaptation · infringement

Use these as practical studio labels, not legal advice:

- **Inspiration** — a direction or tension you name; the source stays outside the composition.
- **Quotation** — a recognisable fragment kept visible as borrowed, with credit.
- **Adaptation** — a named property transformed so the new work can stand without the source image beside it — ledger still required.
- **Infringement** — reproduction that hides provenance, pretends authorship, or strips context for style harvest.

Rabkin’s critique of postmodern fashion adds a second warning: a reference can become a surface sign detached from the garment’s history or material story [(Rabkin 2026)](#ref-torn-rabkin-2026). For each board tile, record not only the visual property you borrow, but also the meaning your new composition claims.

### 4 · Textualise before you design

Before arranging tiles, write three lines naming the properties you seek (edge, volume, rhythm, contrast, material cue). Unlimited visual stimulus can tip boards toward reproduction rather than innovation — that warning remains a teaching hypothesis until bibliographic metadata clears for public citation; the classroom still uses textualisation as a course rule.

### 5 · Emerging: AI speed and provenance

AI retrieval and generation increase the speed and scale of visual reference production while raising provenance, attribution, cultural-appropriation, and stylistic-convergence questions. If you use generated tiles, they enter the ledger as generated inputs with prompt and date — they do not replace the five-field nail or the research question.

**Field note (honest gap):** No source here validates a classroom sequence for mood-boarding as more effective than an alternative. Process evidence (question → ledger → transform → refusal) is graded as practice honesty, not as published proof of pedagogy.

**Open living prompt:** How can a reference remain useful while preserving the source's context, agency, and right not to be reused? Bring forward one source-context note, refusal case, or transformed reference.

## Lab · Portfolio

### Exercise 1 · Audit the search surface {#lab-exercise-1}

**Time:** 15 minutes · **Format:** individual.

**Materials:** notebook or laptop; timer; one search query related to this unit’s CONTENIDOS (compositional sources / fashion references).

**Steps:**

1. Write one research question the eventual board must answer (one sentence).
2. Run one ordinary search and capture the first screen of results (screenshot or written list of the first eight tiles).
3. Name one representational pattern that appears before you choose anything (body type, skin tone, thinness, sexualisation, medical framing, luxury gloss, Euro-only archive, etc.).
4. Write one sentence linking that pattern to Campinho’s finding without claiming the same descriptors.
5. Open one non-Eurocentric archive path named in this unit (FRD or Asian Fashion Archive — the platform-page figures above are navigation aids, not cleared stills) and note one tile or essay title you could ledger without pasting a thumbnail as “free texture.”
6. Keep the note for the portfolio index.

**Portfolio trace:** research question + first-screen capture + pattern name + one sentence on search politics + one non-Eurocentric archive note.

**Judged by:** [Portfolio rubric — process evidence]({{ '/assignments/en/digital-creativity-portfolio/' | relative_url }}#rubric)

**Example trace:** *(Illustrative · not student work.)* Question: “How can evening volume read as structure without thinness as default?” Query “evening volume dress.” First screen: eight thin white bodies, high-gloss studio light. Sentence: “The search already proposed a body before I proposed a silhouette.”

### Exercise 2 · Ledger + one tile transform {#lab-exercise-2}

**Time:** 20 minutes · **Format:** individual; optional 2-minute peer share.

**Materials:** one rights-clear reference image; notebook; timer.

**Steps:**

1. Textualise three sought properties before opening the image pile.
2. Select one tile and complete the five-field ledger (source · reason · cultural position · transformation · refusal).
3. Produce a quick sketch or collage fragment that uses only the named property.
4. Reject any arrangement that hides the source credit.
5. File the tile, the ledger, and the transform side by side. A peer must reconstruct the source from the ledger alone.

**Portfolio trace:** credited tile + five-field ledger + transform fragment + three textualised properties.

**Judged by:** [Portfolio rubric — process evidence]({{ '/assignments/en/digital-creativity-portfolio/' | relative_url }}#rubric)

**Example trace:** *(Illustrative · not student work.)* Properties: stacked horizontal volume; hard void at the hip; matte vs gloss. Property used: stacked horizontal volume. Transform: three paper bands on a flat croquis. Credit: costume plate CC0. Refusal: “I will not paste the historical figure’s face or period accessories.”

**Non-Eurocentric check (Lab 2):** at least one ledger row must name a source that is not a European or US fashion-photography default (diasporic designer, indigenous textile system, Global South archive, or FRD/Asian Fashion Archive entry). If your board cannot do this honestly, the cultural-position field is incomplete.
