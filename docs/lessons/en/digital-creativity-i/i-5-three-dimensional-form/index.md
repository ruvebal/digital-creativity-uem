## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

- **Read a 2D projection for the 3D volume it implies** — before you touch any 3D tool.
- **Describe, in your own words, a sequenced 2D→3D teaching structure** — why a course teaches drawing before prototyping, not the reverse.
- **Produce a small sequence of form studies** that documents how a flat idea became a spatial one.
- **Distinguish "the software rendered a volume" from "I understand the volume"** — the core gap this unit names and does not paper over.

## Why this unit exists: form is taught in a sequence, not absorbed by exposure

**Claim:** three-dimensional form is a spatial-reasoning skill that institutions actually teach as a *sequence* — first 2D drawing and patternmaking literacy, then 3D prototyping layered on top of it — not a software feature a student picks up by using a tool.

This is not a stylistic preference; it is what a real course structure looks like. At the Department of Creative Design & Clothing of the International Hellenic University, 3D virtual prototyping is taught in the third year, sixth semester, *alongside* a 2D CAD patternmaking course — by that point students already know traditional patternmaking, basic construction, and 2D design software. The course itself is split into a theoretical half and a practical/laboratory half (Papahristou & Zolota Tatsi 2024, 5 — Ahmes coat `20e79483`, node `88bcdad6-160b-5fab-a31e-848932abb7c3`).

The same review reports a second, independent case: the Amsterdam Fashion Institute (AMFI) has integrated 3D virtual prototyping into its curriculum for 3rd- and 4th-year students, and that integration itself evolved over time — 3D simulation was initially used only in the final phase of prototyping, then extended to the conceptual phase (Siersema 2015, reported in Papahristou & Zolota Tatsi 2024, 3 — same coat, node `a196bf7e-da78-5714-86df-7e196221f633`).

Read together: two independent institutions structured 3D-form teaching the same way — sequenced after 2D foundations, not instead of them, and refined progressively rather than adopted all at once.

### Declared gap — read this before assuming the sequence is validated

**What is grounded above:** *that* a sequenced 2D→3D teaching structure exists in real fashion-education practice, and *how* two institutions structured it. **What is NOT grounded:** whether that sequence actually produces validated spatial-form understanding. The same review states, as its own finding, that the studies it surveys report *tool-adoption* outcomes — efficiency, visualisation confidence, self-reported creativity — rather than validated measures of form or volume understanding (Papahristou & Zolota Tatsi 2024, 1). This unit teaches the sequence because it is what two real programmes actually do, not because a study has proven the sequence produces better spatial reasoning than an alternative. Hold the two claims apart.

## B1 · Conceptual — magistral 1 h + seminarios 2 h + debates 1 h

### The debate prompt

Put this to the group before the studio piece: **is 3D virtual prototyping a replacement for physical model-making in learning to understand form, or a different representation that has to be taught alongside it, not instead of it?** The IHU/AMFI structure above answers "alongside" with its own curriculum design, sequencing 2D before 3D rather than skipping to 3D. Does that settle the question, or only describe one institution's choice?

### What "understanding form" means, concretely, for this unit

A rendered 3D volume that looks correct is not proof of understanding — the model, not the student, may be doing the spatial reasoning. The minimum evidence trail for this unit:

1. **The 2D read** — before building anything, a short written or sketched statement of what 3D volume a given 2D projection/pattern implies, and why.
2. **The sequence** — sketch → primitive-volume study → simple digital 3D representation, each step visible, not just the final render.
3. **The naming of the gap** — one sentence on where the 2D reading and the 3D result diverged, if they did. "They matched" is a valid, honest answer, but must be stated, not assumed.

## B2 · Studio — talleres 2 h + investigaciones y proyectos 2 h

**Deliverable:** a small sequence of three-dimensional form studies — sketch, a primitive-volume study (built from simple geometric bodies, not a finished garment), and a simple digital 3D representation of the same idea — mirroring the sequenced 2D→3D structure named in B1, at introductory scale.

**Definition of done:**
- A piece ID (a short slug you choose, e.g. `study-01-cone-drape`).
- A process folder holding the sketch, the intermediate volume study, and the final digital representation — kept even if not all of it is published.
- A one-paragraph reflection naming what the 2D reading predicted correctly and what it missed once built in 3D.

**Evidence this deliverable maps to:** Evaluación → **Investigaciones y proyectos (20%)** directly; the process folder and reflection can also support **Cuaderno de prácticas de laboratorio/taller (10%)**. This unit does not invent a third evaluation weight.

Tool docs referenced in this unit (any 2D drawing or 3D modelling package) are dated platform notes, not research citations — check versions before class.

## B3 · Resolución de problemas — 1 h · individual, decontextualised

Not the studio piece. Short problems isolating spatial-form reasoning from software operation:

1. **Diagnostic.** Given three 2D projections of the same object (front, side, top), identify which stated 3D volume they could *not* actually fold into, and say what's inconsistent.
2. Given a rendered 3D mock-up with a visible form/volume error (e.g. a silhouette that doesn't match its stated flat pattern), name the specific inconsistency — not "it looks wrong," but which dimension or projection fails.
3. **No-tool, declared:** without any digital or physical tool, describe in writing how a flat pattern piece becomes a rounded form when draped — name at least two things that change (e.g. ease distribution, grain direction) and why.

Professor answer sketches are not published on this page — kept in the instructor's private notes.
