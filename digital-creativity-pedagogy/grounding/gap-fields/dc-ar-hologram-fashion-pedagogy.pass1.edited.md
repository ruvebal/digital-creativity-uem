Research landscape map · Holograms and augmented reality pedagogy in fashion communication education

Pass 1 · field map · current-state only · cut-off: 16 August 2026

Scope boundary. “AR” below means augmentation of the physical environment rather than fully virtual fashion environments. “Holographic” is retained where institutions/industry use that term, but several contemporary fashion displays marketed as holograms are projection/display illusions rather than true wavefront holography. VR/360 fashion-show studies are included only where they establish phenomena or evaluation constructs; they are not evidence for an AR teaching method. Virtual try-on (VTON/VTO) is treated only as adjacent research, especially for privacy/body-data questions.

## 1. Augmented reality pedagogy in fashion higher education

[EMERGING] State of the art · fashion-specific evidence exists, but remains small and uneven. A 2025 review of emerging technologies in fashion-design HE identifies AR/VR among the predominant immersive technologies being introduced into fashion education, while recent fashion-specific empirical work examines faculty perceptions and student adoption rather than establishing a standardized AR pedagogy. See C. Anderson Carioni Amorim, “Emerging technologies in fashion design higher education…,” Cuadernos del Centro de Estudios de Diseño y Comunicación (2025); and “Pedagogical Efficacy and Barriers: Analysis of Faculty Perceptions on Augmented Reality Integration in Fashion Education” (2025/26). 
SciELO

[EMERGING] Methods currently documented cluster around experiential visualization, spatial inspection, interactive manipulation, project work and iterative prototyping rather than lecture-centred delivery. Recent fashion-education papers attribute perceived value to spatial comprehension and iteration, although this evidence base is not yet large enough to claim a fashion-specific consensus.

## 2. Holographic display and fashion exhibition teaching

[EMERGING] State of the art. The clearest 2025 educational case is FashionTEX/Future Front Row’s Amsterdam event: 12 European fashion schools and 24 emerging designers participated in a student-oriented “holographic fashion show” at Fabrique des Lumières on 20 November 2025, explicitly framed as part of digital transformation in European fashion education. FashionTEX, “Future Front Row × FashionTEX – When Fashion Becomes Light” (2025); UMPRUM, “FashionTex Hologram FashionShow Amsterdam” (2025). 
Fashiontex -

[EMERGING] Pedagogical form documented there is production/exhibition rather than a validated teaching method: student work is translated into digital presentation, supported by digital-fashion experts and staged for a public/professional audience. Independent industry reporting describes students receiving expert support while experimenting with new fashion-communication formats. 
FashionUnited

[ESTABLISHED] The appropriate adjacent scholarly field is digital/immersive fashion exhibition and object-based learning. Smith-Glaviana’s study of 62 university students evaluating a digitally narrated fashion exhibition uses constructivist museum-learning theory and reinforces that digital presentation does not simply reproduce material dress experience. D. Smith-Glaviana, “University Students’ Experience of a Digital Fashion Exhibition…,” Clothing and Textiles Research Journal (online-first 2023; indexed/published volume subsequently), DOI 10.1177/0887302X231161641. 
Sage Journals

[EMERGING] Fashion-museum research is converging on multisensory/kinetic mediation. Colombi’s 2025 discussion of fashion archives notes experimentation with AR, VR, audio, olfactory, haptic and kinetic installations because conventional digitisation loses movement and other sensory properties of dress. C. Colombi, “Fashion archive abilities. The impact of digital technologies…,” European Journal of Cultural Management and Policy (2025), DOI 10.3389/ejcmp.2025.15689. 
Frontiers Partnerships

[ESTABLISHED] Terminological correction. Mixed reality, spatial display, volumetric display, projection-based display, Pepper’s Ghost, digital exhibition, immersive exhibition and phygital exhibition are more precise descriptors than indiscriminate use of “hologram.” Milgram & Kishino’s reality–virtuality continuum remains the canonical basis for distinguishing physical/virtual display configurations: Paul Milgram & Fumio Kishino, “A Taxonomy of Mixed Reality Visual Displays,” IEICE Transactions on Information and Systems E77-D(12), 1994, 1321–1329. 
Transactions Online

[EMERGING] Industry practice supplies cases rather than validated pedagogy. Digital Fashion Week used live motion capture and “holographic” avatar presentation, while the Met’s 2024 Sleeping Beauties exhibition employed animation, AI, scent and Pepper’s Ghost-like techniques to recover otherwise inaccessible dimensions of garments. These document communication possibilities, not educational effectiveness.

## 4. WebXR and mobile AR literacy for fashion communication students

[ESTABLISHED] Technical state of the art. WebXR is now the standards-track web interface for AR/VR device access. The W3C WebXR Augmented Reality Module Level 1 reached Candidate Standard status in April 2025; the core WebXR Device API was a Candidate Standard dated 9 June 2026. W3C, WebXR Augmented Reality Module – Level 1 (2025); W3C, WebXR Device API (2026). 

[EMERGING] The relevant literacy surface is expanding beyond simple image markers. WebXR modules now cover hit testing, depth sensing, anchors, plane detection and lighting estimation, permitting world-anchored objects, occlusion and environmental integration. W3C/Immersive Web WG specifications, 2025–2026.

## 5. Assessing student AR prototypes in design-school curricula

[ESTABLISHED] Assessment is recognized as a distinct XR-design dimension but remains methodologically unsettled. Yang, Zhou & Radu’s XR-Ed design space explicitly includes Assessment alongside physical accessibility, scenario, social interactivity, agency and virtuality degree. Kexin Yang, Xiaofei Zhou & Iulian Radu, “XR-Ed Framework: Designing Instruction-driven and Learner-centered Extended Reality Systems for Education” (2020). 

[ESTABLISHED] Contemporary AR evaluation is dominated by UX/usability instruments rather than disciplinary learning assessment. Graser, Kirschenlohr & Böhm’s systematic review found quantitative approaches dominant, identified only three AR-specific standardized UX questionnaires and explicitly reported no AR-specific UX metric for training and education. Stefan Graser, Felix Kirschenlohr & Stephan Böhm, “User Experience Evaluation of Augmented Reality: A Systematic Literature Review” (2024). 

[EMERGING] Therefore current assessment practice spans different constructs that should not be collapsed: technical functionality, usability, experience, disciplinary communication/design quality and evidence of student learning are separate targets in the literature. XR-Ed and the 2024 AR-UX review make those distinctions visible. 

[EMERGING] Design-education cases increasingly emphasize process, reflection and decision-making as AI/XR enter studio practice. Mendoza-Garcia, Muñoz-Guzman & Sanchez-Ruano describe AI+XR integration at Tecnológico de Monterrey as supporting conceptualization, iteration and discussions around alternatives rather than treating final visualization as the sole outcome. “Binding AI and XR in Design Education: Challenges and Opportunities with Emerging Technologies,” E&PDE 2024. 
Design Society

[ESTABLISHED] Sharper terms. XR learning assessment [DURABLE], performance-based assessment [DURABLE], authentic assessment [DURABLE], prototype evaluation [DURABLE], usability evaluation [DURABLE], presence/immersion measures [DURABLE but construct-specific], and process evidence [DURABLE design-education concept] are analytically preferable to one generic “AR rubric.”

[UNVERIFIED] Fashion-specific blank: no validated assessment instrument was located for undergraduate fashion-communication AR prototypes that jointly establishes construct validity for communication quality, spatial interaction, accessibility, technical robustness and authorship.

[ESTABLISHED] Open problem from AR evaluation research: the absence of education-specific standardized AR UX metrics and the small number of AR-specific instruments are explicitly reported gaps, not inferred ones. 

[EMERGING] Critique: “engagement” or novelty response is an inadequate proxy for learning. Recent HE XR reviews still identify student impact, usability and technical problems separately from instructional outcomes, reinforcing the need not to equate positive experience scores with competence acquisition.

## 8. Accessibility of AR and holographic fashion experiences

[ESTABLISHED] Accessibility is not an optional extension of XR design. W3C’s XR Accessibility User Requirements (XAUR) explicitly defines needs for disabled people across VR, AR and MR, covering alternative input/output and access to immersive content. W3C Accessible Platform Architectures WG, XR Accessibility User Requirements (2021). 

[EMERGING] Recent empirical evidence shows substantial exclusion remains in consumer XR. “Exclusion Rates among Disabled and Older Users of Consumer-level VR and AR Technology,” ACM CHI 2025, directly examines exclusion associated with current consumer VR/AR systems. 
ACM Digital Library

[ESTABLISHED] The broader literature explicitly describes accessibility as under-researched. “Inclusive AR/VR: accessibility barriers for immersive technologies” reports a lack of work examining disability-related barriers across immersive systems; contemporary accessibility studies continue to investigate motor, cognitive and sensory barriers. 
ACM Digital Library

[ESTABLISHED] Relevant design constructs are broader than visual accessibility. XR accessibility encompasses mobility/input requirements, sensory alternatives, orientation, cognitive accessibility, captions/audio description, interaction reach, device comfort and physical access; W3C XAUR provides the durable requirements vocabulary.
