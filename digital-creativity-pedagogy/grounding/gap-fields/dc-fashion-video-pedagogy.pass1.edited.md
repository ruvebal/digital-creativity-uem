Research landscape map · Fashion video editing and moving-image pedagogy in higher education

Scope: higher-education pedagogy, fashion communication/moving image, 2024–2026 priority window. [PLATFORM] denotes software/tool documentation or curricular evidence rather than pedagogical research.

## 1. Fashion video editing education in higher education

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — The clearest fashion-specific pedagogical model remains Kim & Yim’s curriculum for digital fashion-film production. Their curriculum treats moving-image competence as an integrated sequence of digital storytelling, fashion-film conceptualisation, filming, editing and special-effects work rather than as isolated software instruction. Mikyung Kim & Eunhyuk Yim, “Curriculum Design for Digital Fashion Film Making,” Fashion & Textile Research Journal 25(4), 2023, 429–438, DOI 10.5805/SFTI.2023.25.4.429. 
Kftrj

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — Current specialist programmes similarly position editing inside an end-to-end production practice. London College of Fashion’s MA Fashion, Film and Digital Production combines fashion/film theory, research methods, production, digital distribution, live briefs and an independently researched fashion-film project; practice-based workshops remain central. University of the Arts London, “MA Fashion, Film and Digital Production,” current 2026 programme specification. 

[EMERGING] State of the art — Recent general film-education research reinforces a distinction between durable conceptual/artistic learning and automatable technical operations. Monserrat & Srnec’s 2025 film-school study argues that technical automation may follow acquisition of foundational competence, whereas exploratory artistic processes require direct practice, iteration and failure. Ana Laura Monserrat & Nahuel Matias Srnec, “Reflection-AI: artificial intelligence as a redefining force for expressive filmmaking in film schools,” Frontiers in Communication 10, 2025, DOI 10.3389/fcomm.2025.1598376. 
Frontiers

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational — “Fashion film” is itself an unstable hybrid category rather than simply conventional commercial filmmaking applied to clothes. Rees-Roberts traces it through advertising, experimental cinema, documentary, online video and digital promotion; Uhlirova explicitly warns against fixing fashion film too narrowly around branding. Nick Rees-Roberts, Fashion Film: Art and Advertising in the Digital Age, Bloomsbury, 2018; Marketa Uhlirova, “Excavating fashion film: a media archaeological perspective,” Journal of Visual Culture 19(3), 2020, DOI 10.1177/1470412920964915. 
Bloomsbury Publishing

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No substantial 2024–2026 comparative HE study was located that tests alternative methods for teaching editing specifically to fashion-communication students. The 2023 Kim–Yim study remains unusually specific; this blank should therefore be re-searched rather than filled from Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve manuals.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: fashion film, moving-image practice, audiovisual production, post-production, montage/editing, fashion communication. Frontier: AI-assisted post-production, generative filmmaking. The latter appears explicitly in current film-school research rather than constituting a settled fashion-pedagogy taxonomy. 
Kftrj

[ESTABLISHED] Critique — Tool proficiency should not be mistaken for moving-image education. Monserrat & Srnec explicitly distinguish technical processes from artistic concepts/processes and report risks when automation removes the exploratory phases through which students develop cinematic judgement.

## 2. Teaching audiovisual narrative for fashion brands and campaigns

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — Narrative remains an explicit object of fashion-film education, but contemporary programmes combine it with commercial context, audience and distribution. LCF’s current “Narrative Content for Digital Platforms” unit uses a live industry brief and a 360-degree brand campaign while asking students to interrogate fashion narratives rather than merely execute brand assets. UAL, MA Fashion, Film and Digital Production, 2026. 

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — Kim & Yim likewise place “digital storytelling” before or alongside production technique, indicating that fashion-film curricula conceptualise narrative as a design competency rather than a post-production embellishment. Kim & Yim, 2023, DOI 10.5805/SFTI.2023.25.4.429. 
Kftrj

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational — Promotional fashion film does not use one single narrative syntax. Nikola Mijovic’s influential analysis identifies different formal/narrative tendencies in online promotional fashion film and remains one of the load-bearing scholarly references for distinguishing rhetoric, narration and fashion display. Nikola Mijovic, “Narrative form and the rhetoric of fashion in the promotional fashion film,” Film, Fashion & Consumption 2(2), 2013. 
UAL Research Online

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational — Fashion-film scholarship treats clothing not merely as props inside a story but as potentially constitutive of the film’s visual and formal system. This distinction underlies later pedagogical work concerned with communicating fashion concepts through moving images. Mijovic, 2013; Rees-Roberts, 2018. 
UAL Research Online

[EMERGING] Current disciplinary context — Fashion brands increasingly operate across cinematic storytelling, serialised branded entertainment and platform content, making “brand narrative” broader than the traditional campaign film. The scholarly precursor is Rees-Roberts’s analysis of fashion’s transition toward social video and brand content. Nick Rees-Roberts, “After fashion film: social video and brand content in the influencer economy,” Journal of Visual Culture 19(3), 2020, 405–421, DOI 10.1177/1470412920964907. 
Sage Journals

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — I found no 2024–2026 controlled study comparing narrative-learning outcomes from conventional screenplay/storyboard teaching versus platform-first campaign briefs specifically in fashion HE.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: audiovisual narrative, visual storytelling, promotional fashion film, brand storytelling, mise-en-scène. Frontier/contextual: branded entertainment, narrative content for digital platforms, brand world/world-building. Rees-Roberts’s work supports the shift away from “fashion film” as an exhaustive category. 
Sage Journals

[ESTABLISHED] Critique — Equating fashion moving image with advertising is contested inside fashion-film studies. Uhlirova argues that brand-centred definitions obscure historically diverse uses of fashion moving image; media archaeology therefore acts as a methodological corrective to promotional reductionism. Uhlirova, 2020, DOI 10.1177/1470412920964915. 
Sage Journals

## 3. Motion timing and editing literacy in fashion communication courses

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — Fashion-specific curriculum research explicitly includes editing but provides much less granular evidence about how temporal judgement, rhythm or montage are learned. Kim & Yim specify editing and special-effects production within their digital fashion-film curriculum but do not establish a validated fashion-specific theory of editing cognition. Kim & Yim, 2023, DOI 10.5805/SFTI.2023.25.4.429. 
Kftrj

[EMERGING] State of the art — Adjacent audiovisual education is moving toward treating editing rhythm, framing, sound and narrative as meaningful formal variables rather than decorative production choices. Jonas’s 2026 “Cinematic Instructional Design” synthesis names editing rhythm, sound design, narrative structure and framing as variables affecting attention and engagement, although its domain is instructional video rather than fashion-production teaching. J. Jonas, “Cinematic Instructional Design: Toward a Theory of Visual…,” TechTrends, 2026. 

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational — Fashion-film form cannot be reduced to conventional continuity storytelling. Mijovic and later fashion-film scholarship identify narrative, non-narrative and formally fashion-led modes; this makes montage/rhythm literacy particularly relevant because meaning may reside in visual organisation rather than plot alone. Mijovic, 2013; Rees-Roberts, 2018. 
UAL Research Online

[EMERGING] Adjacent HE evidence — Assessment research now examines post-production filmmaking itself as a distinctive epistemic practice rather than merely a vehicle for academic content. Helena Florén, “Assessing film in higher education: straddling academic …,” Learning, Media and Technology, 2025, DOI 10.1080/17439884.2025.2471405, studies assessment of a post-production film project in Swedish vocationally oriented HE. 
Taylor & Francis Online

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No verifiable 2024–2026 fashion-HE experiment was found that measures student acquisition of timing, pacing, continuity/discontinuity, rhythmic montage or shot-duration judgement.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: montage, continuity editing, rhythmic editing, temporal structure, shot duration, audiovisual rhythm. Fashion-specific but less stable: fashion-film aesthetics, fashion-led/organic narrative. Mijovic remains a useful bridge between generic film terminology and fashion-specific formal analysis. 
UAL Research Online

[UNVERIFIED] Critique/blank — Claims that rapid social-media consumption requires abandoning classical montage literacy could not be substantiated as an established pedagogical position in fashion HE. No verifiable source found; do not treat this as field consensus.

## 4. Social video versus fashion film pedagogy

[ESTABLISHED] State of the field — Specialists have already challenged “fashion film” as an adequate umbrella for contemporary brand moving image. Rees-Roberts argues that fashion’s moving-image ecology increasingly includes social video, influencer content and branded entertainment, making format and circulation platform analytically important. Rees-Roberts, “After fashion film,” 2020, DOI 10.1177/1470412920964907. 
Sage Journals

[ESTABLISHED] Current curricular evidence nevertheless shows coexistence rather than replacement. LCF continues to teach “Fashion Film Production” while separately teaching “Narrative Content for Digital Platforms,” indicating a distinction between the cinematic fashion-film object and platform-distributed campaign communication. UAL, MA Fashion, Film and Digital Production, current 2026 curriculum. 

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational — Fashion film historically spans far more than today's vertical/social formats. Uhlirova’s media archaeology retrieves fashion moving-image practices from 1900–1929 and argues that current digital definitions should not erase this longer heterogeneous history. Uhlirova, 2020, DOI 10.1177/1470412920964915; Archaeology of Fashion Film project, Central Saint Martins/UAL. 
Sage Journals

[EMERGING] Named research locus — The Central Saint Martins/UAL Fashion in Film / Archaeology of Fashion Film research lineage remains important precisely because it studies fashion moving image across media history rather than treating contemporary platforms as unprecedented. Elizabeth Caston’s 2024 retrospective describes the Archaeology of Fashion Film project as the first systematic study of the silent-era history of fashion film. 
openscreensjournal.com

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No 2024–2026 fashion-education study was located that directly compares student learning from vertical short-form/social-video production against longer-form fashion-film production.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: fashion film, promotional film, branded content. Frontier/platform-sensitive: social video, creator content, short-form video, platform-native video, vertical video. Rees-Roberts’s “social video and brand content” is the sharper scholarly vocabulary for the post-fashion-film shift. 
Sage Journals

[ESTABLISHED] Critique — A platform-only framing risks historical presentism. Uhlirova’s media-archaeological argument explicitly resists the idea that digital branded video exhausts what fashion film is or has been. 
Sage Journals

## 5. AI video generation and student authorship in fashion schools

[EMERGING] State of the art — Direct fashion-video pedagogy evidence remains scarce, but 2025 film-school research now supplies empirical classroom data. Monserrat & Srnec studied a 2024 cinematography classroom experiment plus surveys of audiovisual students; by February 2025, AI use among the surveyed cohort had increased substantially, while concerns about copyright, over-reliance and loss of human creativity persisted. DOI 10.3389/fcomm.2025.1598376. 
Frontiers

[EMERGING] State of the art — Students in that study judged generative visual outputs as frequently “too polished,” generic/hegemonic or inauthentic, even while accepting text-oriented AI more readily. This is useful evidence against assuming that visual GenAI is uncontroversially welcomed by emerging image-makers. Monserrat & Srnec, 2025. 
Frontiers

[EMERGING] Fashion-education evidence converges on process rather than output quantity. An & Park’s interviews with ten university fashion instructors found support for process-oriented qualitative assessment, reflection, critical thinking and explicit human creative intervention when AI is used. Hyosun An & Minjung Park, “AI tools and fashion design education: instructor perspectives on student challenges and design process tool support,” Fashion and Textiles 13, 2026, article 3, DOI 10.1186/s40691-025-00452-9. 

[EMERGING] Studio-design research makes authorship opacity a central assessment problem. Hyunyim Park reports that GenAI can obscure learning processes, diffuse ethical responsibility and undermine creative agency and dialogic critique; the paper consequently foregrounds process transparency and ethical human–AI co-creation. “Generative AI in Studio-Based Design Education: Human–AI Relationships, Collaboration and Assessment,” IASDR 2025, DOI 10.21606/iasdr.2025.845. 

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational for current AI discussion — AI use in creative education should be distinguished between assistance in technical/repetitive processes and substitution for artistic decision-making. Both Monserrat & Srnec and An & Park explicitly preserve a human role in creative judgement rather than treating automation as pedagogically neutral. 
Frontiers

[EMERGING] Named research loci — Escuela Nacional de Experimentación y Realización Cinematográfica (ENERC, Argentina) and the University Film and Video Association are directly represented in Monserrat & Srnec’s film-school AI study; Hong Kong Polytechnic University School of Design is represented in Park’s studio-based GenAI assessment work. 
Frontiers

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No 2024–2026 peer-reviewed fashion-communication classroom study was located that isolates text-to-video systems specifically and tests their effects on fashion-film authorship, editing competence or assessment validity.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Frontier: AI audiovisual literacy, generative filmmaking, human–AI co-creation, process transparency, AI-mediated studio learning. Monserrat & Srnec explicitly cite “AI Audiovisual Literacy” as an emerging curricular framing. 
Frontiers

[EMERGING] Controversy — The field is not divided simply into “adopt” versus “ban.” Empirical film and design education studies document simultaneous use, resistance, authenticity concerns and perceived creative value; this ambivalence is itself part of the current research object. 
Frontiers

## 6. Assessing process evidence in fashion editing briefs

[EMERGING] State of the art — Process visibility is becoming more important under GenAI because polished outputs no longer reliably disclose how learning occurred. Park’s 2025 studio-design study identifies increasing opacity of learning processes as an assessment problem and proposes process transparency as a response. DOI 10.21606/iasdr.2025.845. 

[EMERGING] Fashion-specific corroboration — Instructors interviewed by An & Park explicitly preferred qualitative evaluation of creative engagement and AI use over simply counting or judging generated outputs. Reflection, critical thinking and creative intervention are identified as assessment-relevant dimensions. An & Park, 2026, DOI 10.1186/s40691-025-00452-9. 

[EMERGING] Adjacent moving-image assessment research is now examining film production as an assessable knowledge practice in its own right. Florén’s 2025 study specifically investigates assessment of a post-production film project in a vocationally oriented HE media programme rather than assuming conventional essay criteria transfer cleanly to filmmaking. DOI 10.1080/17439884.2025.2471405. 
Taylor & Francis Online

[ESTABLISHED] Current specialist fashion-film programmes already combine final artefact production with research and critical reflection. LCF’s 60-credit Masters Project requires an independent/original fashion-film project underpinned by deep research and critical reflection. UAL, current 2026 programme description. 

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No validated fashion-video-specific rubric was found for edit histories, timeline/version evidence, rushes, decision logs or AI provenance. Such requirements may exist institutionally, but I found no peer-reviewed field standard establishing them.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: process-based assessment, reflective practice, formative critique, practice-based assessment. Frontier: process transparency, AI provenance, human–AI contribution disclosure. 

[EMERGING] Critique — Finished-product assessment becomes methodologically weaker when AI-mediated workflows make the relation between visible artefact and student learning opaque. This problem is stated directly in current studio-design research rather than merely inferred from plagiarism debates. Park, 2025. 

## 7. Sound design basics for fashion moving-image curricula

[ESTABLISHED] State of the broader field — Sound education is best understood as audiovisual design rather than an ancillary technical layer. The interdisciplinary Brazilian HE programme Sound, Image and Motion explicitly combines visual arts, audiovisual work and sound creation. André de Souza Stolfi et al., “Sound, Image and Motion: Teaching with an Interdisciplinary Approach,” Organised Sound, 2024. 
Cambridge University Press

[EMERGING] State of the art — A 2024 teaching report on audiovisual sound design describes project-based production involving musical creation, image synchronisation, dubbing and audio processing; it also identifies the steep technical learning curve of audio tools as a pedagogical obstacle. Miguel Civit & Francisco Cuadrado, “Diseño de sonido para producciones audiovisuales e historias sonoras en el aula…,” 2024. 

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational — Sound-design pedagogy for novices has long emphasised listening and conceptual attention before or alongside software operation. Karen Collins et al., “Introducing Students to Sound,” documents exercises developed across approximately a decade of teaching students without previous sound training. 
Research Catalogue

[ESTABLISHED] Fashion-specific blank — Kim & Yim’s fashion-film curriculum verifies editing, filming, storytelling and special effects, but the accessible abstract does not establish sound design as a separately theorised curricular component. Kim & Yim, 2023. 
Kftrj

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No 2024–2026 peer-reviewed study was located specifically on teaching music editing, dialogue, ambience, Foley, synchronisation or sound-image relations to fashion-communication students.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: sound design, audio post-production, sound-image relationship, synchronisation, diegetic/non-diegetic sound, sound editing. Frontier: AI-assisted sound design, text-to-audio. The latter is beginning to appear in tertiary creative-audio pedagogy but is not yet fashion-specific.

[ESTABLISHED] State of the field — Contemporary fashion moving image circulates through both cinematic and platform-native ecologies; scholarship therefore does not support treating one as a complete substitute for the other. Rees-Roberts identifies the expansion from fashion film to social video/brand content, while current LCF provision continues to teach fashion film alongside digital-platform narrative. Rees-Roberts, 2020, DOI 10.1177/1470412920964907; UAL, 2026.

[ESTABLISHED] Durable layer — Narrative organisation, visual form, editing, sound and the relationship between clothing and moving image pre-date any contemporary editing application or social platform. Mijovic, Uhlirova and Rees-Roberts provide the canonical fashion-film scholarship supporting this longer formal history. 
UAL Research Online

LCF describes production processes, research, storytelling and digital distribution without defining expertise through Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve certification. 

[UNVERIFIED] Blank — No fashion-HE study located in the 2024–2026 search directly tests whether platform-specific editing instruction transfers less well than platform-agnostic montage instruction.

## 10. Accessibility, captions and inclusive moving-image deliverables

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — Captioning is a baseline moving-image accessibility requirement, not an optional stylistic enhancement. W3C’s Web Accessibility Initiative specifies synchronised captions for prerecorded video with meaningful audio at WCAG Level A and live captions at Level AA. W3C WAI, “Captions/Subtitles,” current WCAG media guidance. 

[ESTABLISHED] State of the art — Accessibility research argues that captions benefit populations beyond Deaf and hard-of-hearing users, while automated captions require quality control. Locke et al.’s HE research identifies benefits for disabled and multilingual learners and frames ASR captioning as inclusive provision; Pérez-Arteaga et al. (2025) directly study students’ evaluation of caption quality in educational video. 
puntoorginternationaljournal.org

[EMERGING] State of the art — Current accessibility work is moving beyond a one-size-fits-all subtitle model. Research involving people with aphasia identifies continuing barriers in conventional subtitles and calls for more adaptable, user-sensitive media-access design. Zihao You & Michael Crabb, “Subtitled Media Adaptations for People with Aphasia…,” 2025. 

[ESTABLISHED] Foundational pedagogical critique — Accessibility can be taught as part of media authorship from project inception rather than as end-stage compliance. Chelsea Temple Jones, Kimberlee Collins & Anne Zbitnew’s project-based media pedagogy paired students with disabled media makers and explicitly reframed access as an aesthetic/production concern. “Accessibility as Aesthetic in Broadcast Media: Critical Access Theory and Disability Justice as Project-Based Learning,” DOI 10.1177/10776958211000198. 
ResearchGate

[EMERGING] Current HE evidence confirms that captions alone do not exhaust audiovisual accessibility. Research with visually impaired students identifies captions, audio description and alternative media formats as relevant components of equitable video access. “Using videos in higher education: Experiences of students …,” 2025. 
Sage Journals

[UNVERIFIED] Fashion-specific blank — No 2024–2026 peer-reviewed study was located evaluating whether fashion-communication students are systematically taught captions, audio description or accessible social-video delivery.

[ESTABLISHED] Terminology — Durable: closed captions, subtitles for the Deaf and hard of hearing (SDH), audio description, media accessibility, WCAG. More critical/pedagogical: critical access, accessibility as aesthetic, disability justice. 

[ESTABLISHED] Critique — Automatic captions are not equivalent to accessible captions. Contemporary caption research continues to identify accuracy and contextual failures in ASR; experimental work is investigating LLM-assisted correction precisely because raw automatic transcription remains inadequate in many cases. Fathallah, Bhole & Staab, “Empowering the Deaf and Hard of Hearing Community: Enhancing Video Captions Using Large Language Models,” 2024. 

[ESTABLISHED] Critique — Treating accessibility exclusively as final-stage remediation is specifically challenged within media pedagogy. Jones, Collins & Zbitnew argue from a project-based case that incorporating access planning at production inception changes both technical practice and attitudes toward disability. DOI 10.1177/10776958211000198. 
ResearchGate
